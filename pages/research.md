Historically, nearly all astronomy is performed using **light**, ripples in the electromagnetic field due to moving electrons.
Astronomers use light in all its varieties, from long-wavelength radio waves to high-energy gamma rays, to access and explore diverse phenomena across all astrophysical and cosmological scales.
There remain, however, **regions of the Universe that we cannot see**.
Examples include
* **Black holes**, punctures in spacetime that by definition emit no light
* The deep **interiors of massive stars**
* The birth of the Universe in the **Big Bang**, currently shrouded behind the Cosmic Microwave Background

Light is not our only tool for studying the Universe, however.
Just as the motion of electrons creates electromagnetic waves (light), the motion of mass creates **gravitational waves, ripples in the very fabric of our four-dimensional spacetime**.
Gravitational waves encode information about objects that are difficult or otherwise impossible to see, either because they are completely dark (black holes) or completely masked (like the Big Bang), and thus promise to open an entirely window through which to study our Universe.
Gravitational waves were largely a theoretical curiosity for almost a century, following their first prediction by Einstein.
In 2015, however, the **Advanced LIGO** (Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory) experiment successfully detected gravitational waves from a nearly **light-speed collision between two black holes** (called a binary black hole merger).
In the ten years since, the LIGO experiment has witnessed hundreds of additional black hole mergers, as well as collisions involving neutron stars (effectively massive atomic nuclei the size of Williamstown and with the mass of our Sun).
